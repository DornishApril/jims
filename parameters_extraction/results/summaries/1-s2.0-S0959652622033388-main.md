## SECTION 4: POWER GENERATION ALGORITHM — EXACT EXTRACTION (STRICT EXTRACTION)

### 4.1 Dispatch Strategy

- **Type of dispatch:** Rule-based energy management strategy (priority-based with conditional logic)
- **Priority order:**
  1. Renewable generation (PV + Wind) serves electrical load first
  2. Excess energy serves RO desalination demand
  3. Remaining excess goes to electrolyzer for hydrogen production
  4. Hydrogen tank stores produced H2
  5. During deficit: fuel cell converts stored H2 to electricity
  6. During deficit below FC capability: biomass generator provides backup (if load >= 30% of BG rated power)
  7. Any remaining excess after all storage full: dumped via resistance heater

### 4.2 Power Flow Logic

**When ENet(t) > 0 (renewable generation > electrical demand):**
- Electrical demand fully served
- Excess energy (EExcess = ENet - EElec) directed to RO desalination
- If EExcess > ERO: RO demand fully met, remaining surplus to electrolyzer for H2 production
- If EExcess < ERO: stored H2 discharged via FC to meet remaining RO demand
- When H2 tank at max capacity and all demands met: excess dumped via resistance heater

**When ENet(t) = 0 (generation = demand):**
- Renewable production meets both electrical and water demands
- RO demand supported by stored hydrogen via fuel cell

**When ENet(t) < 0 (generation < demand):**
- Fuel cell meets electrical deficit by discharging H2 tank
- If deficit persists and load >= 30% of BG rated power: biomass gasifier starts
- If load < 30% of BG rated power: classified as unmet load (BG does not start)

### 4.3 Control Parameters

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| **BG minimum load threshold** | 30% of rated power (below this = unmet load) |
| **Fuel cell efficiency** | 50% |
| **Inverter efficiency** | 96% |
| **H2 higher heating value** | 37.8 kWh/kg |
| **RO specific energy consumption** | 4.38 kWh/m3 |

| **PV derating factor** | 88% |
| **PV temperature coefficient** | -0.35%/C |
| **Wind turbine cut-in** | 2.75 m/s |
| **Wind turbine rated** | 6.5 m/s |
| **Wind turbine cut-out** | 20 m/s |

### 4.4 Algorithm Flow

1. **Input:** Solar irradiation, wind speed, temperature, electrical load demand, RO water demand (hourly data for 8760 hours)
2. **Calculate renewable output:** PV output (Eq. 5-6) and Wind output (Eq. 1-4)
3. **Compute net energy:** ENet(t) = EPV(t) + EWT(t) - EElec(t)
4. **If ENet(t) > 0:** Serve load, meet RO demand, electrolyze H2, store in tank, dump remaining
5. **If ENet(t) = 0:** Serve load, FC supports RO if needed
6. **If ENet(t) < 0:** FC discharges to meet deficit; BG starts if load >= 30% rated; else unmet
7. **Power balance equation:** EPV(t) + EWT(t) + EBG(t) + EFC(t) - EElectrolyzer(t) = EL(t) + EExcess(t)
8. **H2 balance:** HHS(t) = HHS(t-1) + HElectrolyzer(t) [surplus] or HHS(t-1) - HFC(t) [deficit]

## SECTION 5: ANALYTICAL INSIGHT AND SYSTEM EXPLANATION (YOUR ANALYSIS)

### 5.1 Power Generation Walkthrough

**Typical day for the best configuration (PV/WT/BG/FC):**

**Early morning (5:00-7:00):** Solar irradiation is near zero. Wind may contribute modestly (annual average only 3.01 m/s). The biomass generator (150 kW rated) covers the morning load peak as residents wake up, turn on fans, water pumps, and lights. The BG operates above its 30% threshold (45 kW minimum), so it runs efficiently. The fuel cell may supplement if BG output is insufficient.

**Midday (10:00-14:00):** Solar peaks dramatically — the 170 kW PV array generates near-rated output (accounting for 88% derating factor and temperature losses). With 264,956 kWh/yr from PV, average daily PV generation is approximately 726 kWh/day. During peak sun hours, PV alone can exceed the community's electrical demand. The excess energy first serves the RO desalination system (131.4 kWh/day needed), then the surplus powers the 42 kW electrolyzer to produce hydrogen. The electrolyzer converts excess electricity to H2 at a rate governed by Eq. (9): approximately 0.0239 kg/kWh (linear term dominates at low power). With roughly 200-300 kWh of excess during peak hours, the system produces approximately 5-7 kg of hydrogen per day.

**Evening (17:00-20:00):** Solar drops rapidly as load peaks (residential evening demand — lights, TVs, fans). The biomass generator ramps up to cover the gap. The fuel cell (30 kW) supplements, consuming stored hydrogen. The H2 consumption rate is governed by Eq. (11): HFC = EFC / (0.50 x 37.8) = EFC / 18.9 kWh/kg. If the FC operates at 20 kW for 3 evening hours (60 kWh), it consumes approximately 3.2 kg of H2.

**Night (21:00-5:00):** No solar. Wind may contribute (152,176 kWh/yr = ~417 kWh/day average, but nighttime wind is typically lower). The biomass generator runs steadily at moderate load. The fuel cell handles transient peaks. If load drops below 30% of BG rated power (45 kW), the BG shuts off and the FC takes over — any remaining deficit below FC capacity becomes unmet load (4,919 kWh/yr = ~13.5 kWh/day average unmet).

**Seasonal variations:** Summer (March-October) has higher daily load (1,992 kWh/day) due to fan usage. Winter (November-February) has lower load (1,535 kWh/day). Solar resource varies seasonally (Fig. 4 shows monthly irradiation patterns). The system is sized to meet summer peak demand, meaning winter operation has more excess energy available for hydrogen production and desalination.

### 5.2 System Behavior Analysis

**Why PV/WT/BG/FC was chosen as optimal:**

The multi-objective optimization (minimize COE + minimize human health damage) found that including biomass as a dispatchable backup dramatically reduces the need for oversized fuel cell and hydrogen storage. The BG provides reliable baseload at night and during low-renewable periods, reducing the FC requirement from 226 kW (PV/WT/FC only) to just 30 kW — an 87% reduction. This is economically crucial because fuel cells cost $3,000/kW capital with a short 5-year lifetime.

The relationship between renewable penetration and storage sizing is inverse: without BG (PV/WT/FC), the system must be massively oversized (811 kW PV, 910 kW wind, 226 kW FC, 1068 kg H2) to ensure reliability during low-resource periods. This oversizing creates 1,392,700 kWh/yr of excess energy (49.7% of total generation!), making the system only 28.38% efficient and driving COE to $0.69/kWh.

**Key trade-offs identified:**
- Adding wind reduces FC, H2 tank, and electrolyzer sizes by 21%, 33%, and 65% respectively
- Removing wind increases PV by 43% but reduces excess energy by 72%
- BG integration reduces COE from $0.69 to $0.20/kWh but adds biomass fuel costs and emissions
- The PV/WT/BG/FC system achieves the best balance: lowest COE ($0.1967/kWh), lowest emissions (56,345 kg CO2-eq/yr), and acceptable social metrics

**Edge cases:** During extended cloudy/calm periods, the system relies on stored hydrogen and biomass. The 24 kg H2 tank provides approximately 907 kWh of recoverable energy (24 kg x 37.8 kWh/kg x 50% FC efficiency = 454 kWh net). This can supply ~50% of average daily load for roughly half a day before BG must take over. If biomass fuel supply is interrupted, the system would rely solely on FC and face potential unmet load.

### 5.3 Critical Evaluation

**Reasonableness of assumptions:**
- The LPSP constraint of 1 +/- 0.5% is reasonable for a remote community system
- NASA solar/wind data is standard practice but may not capture microclimate effects
- The assumption that excess energy improves HDI (Eq. 26) is a simplification — actual socioeconomic impact depends on how communities use the energy
- No battery storage is unusual; the hydrogen-only storage approach is environmentally motivated (avoiding battery disposal in the Sundarbans) but adds conversion losses

**Limitations:**
- The study assumes perfect forecasting of renewable output (deterministic optimization)
- Biomass availability is assumed constant (no seasonal variation in fuel supply)
- The model does not account for component degradation over time
- No battery means no fast-response storage for transient load events
- The HDI calculation is a rough proxy based on energy availability, not actual socioeconomic data
- Carbon emissions from biomass combustion are counted as lifecycle emissions (manufacturing/transport), not operational — this is debatable since biogas CO2 is often considered carbon-neutral

**Generalizability:**
- The methodology (NSGA-II + fuzzy decision-making + H2 storage + RO desalination) is transferable to other remote coastal communities
- The specific optimal configuration is unique to Mousuni Island's resources and load profile
- The finding that BG integration dramatically reduces cost is likely generalizable to areas with biomass availability

**What would change with different parameters:**
- If fuel cell costs decrease (as projected), the PV/WT/FC configuration could become competitive
- If carbon taxes increase significantly, the low-emission advantage of PV/WT/BG/FC becomes more valuable
- If wind resources were better (higher than 3.01 m/s average), wind penetration would increase
- If battery costs drop below hydrogen storage costs, a battery-hybrid approach might be preferable

### 5.5 Key Takeaways

1. **Biomass integration is the single most impactful design choice:** Adding a 150 kW biomass generator reduces COE from $0.69/kWh to $0.20/kWh (71% reduction) by eliminating the need for massive overbuilding of renewable and storage capacity. The BG provides reliable nighttime and backup power that would otherwise require 7x more fuel cell capacity and 45x more hydrogen storage.

2. **Hydrogen-only storage (no batteries) is viable but costly:** The electrolyzer-fuel cell storage chain works but introduces significant conversion losses (electricity -> H2 -> electricity at ~50% round-trip efficiency). The system compensates by using biomass as the primary backup, with hydrogen handling short-term transients and RO energy buffering.

3. **The system achieves true multi-objective optimization:** By simultaneously minimizing COE ($0.1967/kWh) and human health damage (7.89E-2 DALYs), the study demonstrates that environmental and economic goals can align. The best configuration also has the lowest emissions (56,345 kg CO2-eq/yr), lowest ecosystem damage (4.47E-4 species-year), and positive social impact (2.484 jobs, HDI 0.5885).

4. **Excess energy is a resource, not waste:** The system productively uses excess renewable generation for freshwater production (30 m3/day via RO) and hydrogen storage. Only 49,960 kWh/yr (5.4%) is truly dumped, demonstrating effective energy cascading: electricity -> water -> hydrogen storage -> electricity.

5. **The Sundarbans context drives unique design choices:** The environmental sensitivity of the mangrove ecosystem motivates avoiding diesel generators and batteries. The locally available biomass (agricultural waste) provides a dispatchable renewable backup. The result is a system that simultaneously addresses energy poverty, water scarcity, and environmental conservation in one integrated solution.
