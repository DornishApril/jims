## SECTION 4: POWER GENERATION ALGORITHM — EXACT EXTRACTION (STRICT EXTRACTION)

### 4.1 Dispatch Strategy

- **Type:** Time-series chronological simulation with hourly resolution
- **Priority order:** PV generation serves load directly during daylight hours; excess PV energy charges batteries; battery discharges to meet load deficit when PV generation is insufficient
- **Decision logic:** SOC-based — if SOC reaches Bmax (80%), charging stops; if SOC reaches Bmin (20%), load is disconnected (supply outage occurs)
- **Operating constraint:** Battery SOC must remain between 20% and 80% to extend battery life

### 4.2 Power Flow Logic

- **State 1 (PV generation ≥ Load demand):** Excess energy charges battery bank — Battery energy updates: `EB(t) = EB(t-1) + (EPV(t) - EL(t)/ηinv) × ηbatt.in`
- **State 2 (PV generation < Load demand):** Battery discharges to meet deficit — Battery energy updates: `EB(t) = EB(t-1) - (EL(t)/ηinv - EPV(t))`
- **Loss of Power Supply (LPS):** Occurs when PV generation + battery energy cannot satisfy load demand: `LPS(t) = EL(t) - (EPV(t) + (EB(t-1) - EBmin) × ηinv)`
- **LPSP definition:** `LPSP = Σ LPS(t) / Σ EL(t)` over the entire year
- **MPPT Control:** Perturb and Observe (P&O) algorithm — measures Vpv and Ipv, calculates Ppv, compares with previous values, adjusts Vmppt direction based on sign of Vchange × Pchange
- **Inverter:** 48 V DC to 415 V three-phase AC, 50 Hz

### 4.3 Control Parameters

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Battery SOC minimum (Bmin) | **20%** |
| Battery SOC maximum (Bmax) | **80%** |
| Battery charging efficiency | **80%** |
| Inverter efficiency (ηinv) | **90%** |
| MPPT converter efficiency | **90%** |
| Simulation time step | **1 hour** |
| Simulation duration | **365 days (8,760 hours)** |
| STC irradiance | 1,000 W/m² |
| STC temperature | 25°C |

### 4.4 Algorithm Flow

1. **Input data:** Hourly solar irradiance (NASA), hourly temperature (NASA), hourly load profile (FKE Building measurement)
2. **PV power calculation:** Single diode model with temperature correction (Eqs. 4–5) → hourly maximum power per PV array
3. **Configuration sweep:** Iterate from (1 battery, 1 PV) to (300 batteries, 300 PV arrays) — 90,000 total configurations
4. **For each configuration:** Hour-by-hour simulation for 8,760 hours
   - Calculate PV generation for hour t
   - If PV ≥ Load → State 1 (charge battery, cap at Bmax)
   - If PV < Load → State 2 (discharge battery, floor at Bmin)
   - If battery at Bmin and load still unmet → record LPS
5. **LPSP calculation:** Sum LPS / Sum Load for the year
6. **Isoreliability line extraction:** Identify all configurations yielding same LPSP (10%, 1%, 0.1%, 0.01%)
7. **Optimization:** GCM finds tangent; LCC finds minimum cost; RIM traces LCC gradient to find point of diminishing returns

## SECTION 5: ANALYTICAL INSIGHT AND SYSTEM EXPLANATION (YOUR ANALYSIS)

### 5.1 Power Generation Walkthrough

This SAPS is designed for the FKE Building at UTM, a large academic facility with peak demands driven primarily by air-conditioning. The system's daily operation follows a predictable solar-driven rhythm:

**Morning (7–9 a.m.):** Solar irradiance begins rising. The 21–22 PV arrays (each rated at 7.875 kW per the 5×5 configuration of Sunpower 315W modules) start generating. However, morning irradiance is relatively low, so PV output may be only 20–30% of rated capacity (~457 kW peak potential at noon, but perhaps ~100–200 kW in early morning). This is insufficient to meet the building's load which ramps during this period (students arriving, laboratories operating). The deficit is covered by battery discharge. The 57–62 battery banks, each storing 10 kWh of usable energy (operating between 20% and 80% SOC), collectively can deliver up to 570–620 kWh of usable energy — roughly equivalent to 9–10 hours of the 62.2 kW peak load, or about 7.5 hours at the average load.

**Midday (10 a.m.–2 p.m.):** Solar generation peaks. By the paper's example data for day 20, the 22 PV arrays produce a maximum hourly output of 154.5 kWh during one hour — suggesting an instantaneous PV power of ~154.5 kW in that hour. Total daily generation was 1,310.9 kWh from 22 arrays. The daily load is 813 kWh, meaning the system has a substantial midday surplus. This excess (~497 kWh on a good day) charges the battery bank. Batteries, which discharged during the morning ramp, begin recharging. The battery reaches full charge (80% SOC) around 1 p.m. according to the data from day 20.

**Evening (2–10 p.m.):** Solar generation declines. The building still carries load from laboratory sessions (2–4 p.m., 42–64 kW) and replacement classes (5–10 p.m., ~32 kW). As PV output fades, the battery bank takes over entirely. The MPPT converter continues tracking maximum power from panels until irradiance becomes negligible.

**Night (11 p.m.–6 a.m.):** Zero PV generation. The building's base load of ~16 kW is met entirely by battery discharge. With 57–62 battery banks operating down to 20% SOC, the system can survive extended nighttime periods. The battery reaches its minimum SOC of 20% around 7 a.m. at the end of the longest night.

**Seasonal variations:** Solar irradiance peaks in February–March and reaches its minimum in December. Temperatures vary between 30.9°C (December) and 33.5°C (March), affecting PV efficiency negatively at higher temperatures (negative Voc temperature coefficient). The worst-case days (e.g., day 288 simulation) show PV generation dropping to 1,090.6 kWh with a maximum hourly output of only 168.6 kWh, potentially triggering a supply outage on subsequent days when battery reserves are already depleted.

### 5.2 System Behavior Analysis

**Why the GCM and LCC produce the same configuration (21 PV arrays, 57 battery banks):** Both methods optimize within the same isoreliability constraint of ~1% LPSP (actually 0.812% due to integer component counts). The GCM minimizes capital cost by geometric tangency; the LCC explicitly computes lifetime cost. The LCC reveals that adding one more PV array (going from 20 to 21) reduces the required battery banks from 125 to 57 — a 78% reduction — saving RM 470,031 in LCC. This is because PV arrays directly reduce the energy that must be stored, and since batteries need replacement every 7 years (vs. PV at 25 years), the battery-dominated configuration is far more expensive over the system lifetime despite lower initial component count.

**Why the RIM recommends 22 PV arrays and 62 battery banks:** The key insight is the nonlinear relationship between LPSP and LCC. Reducing LPSP from 1% to 0.18% only increases LCC by 4.9% (RM 1,512,771 to RM 1,587,139). This is the "knee" of the cost-reliability curve — a region where reliability improves dramatically (77.8% reduction in unmet energy) for a negligible cost increment. Beyond 0.18% LPSP, the LCC increases exponentially (RM 1,669,170 → RM 2,176,280 for further reduction to 0.17%), meaning each additional unit of reliability becomes progressively more expensive.

**The role of the isoreliability line shape:** The paper identifies three regions on each isoreliability line: (1) the constant PV array region (18–22 arrays, where adding batteries doesn't help because generation is the bottleneck), (2) the constant battery region (38–174 batteries depending on LPSP, where adding PV doesn't help because storage is the bottleneck), and (3) the optimum sizing region between them where both quantities vary together. The key insight is that the isoreliability lines for different LPSP values are not parallel — they curve in ways that create pockets of high cost-efficiency.

**The impact of Malaysia's equatorial climate:** As a non-seasonal tropical country, Malaysia experiences consistent solar irradiance year-round (NASA data: 4.21–5.56 kWh/m²/day annually). This makes LPSP a more appropriate reliability metric than "Days of Autonomy" (DOA), which is designed for seasonal climates where extended cloudy periods are the main sizing driver.

### 5.3 Critical Evaluation

**Reasonable assumptions:** The 20–80% SOC operating range for lead-acid batteries is realistic for extending cycle life. The 1-hour time step in chronological simulation is standard practice. The NASA meteorological data source is widely accepted for tropical regions. The choice of 1% LPSP as the target is more conservative than the 10% recommended in literature.

**Limitations:**
1. Only weekday load profiles are used — weekend load patterns (likely lower) are not considered, potentially oversizing the system
2. The paper acknowledges that actual LPSP (0.812%) differs from the target (1%) due to integer constraint on components
3. No degradation model for PV panels over the 25-year lifetime
4. Battery replacement schedule based on fixed 7-year lifetime, not actual cycling degradation
5. No economic discussion of inflation or variable interest rates
6. The P&O MPPT algorithm's limitations under partial shading are acknowledged
7. System is sized for a single building — general scaling to other loads is not discussed

**Generalizability:** The RIM approach itself is generalizable to any stand-alone PV system. The specific component configuration is not — it depends heavily on local solar resource, load profile shape, and component costs.

**What would change if parameters differed?**
- Higher load growth → would require future expansion analysis
- Battery technology change (lithium-ion instead of lead-acid) → deeper DoD (e.g., 10–90%), longer life, higher efficiency → would shift optimal PV/battery tradeoff toward more PV, fewer battery banks
- Grid connection possibility → would eliminate the need for extreme reliability sizing; could use grid as backup
- Different location with seasonal variation → would need different sizing approach accounting for worst-month irradiance

**Comparison with literature:** The paper compares against Research Work 1 (Nordin & Rahman 2016, rural house, 2 kWh/day load, LCC = RM 20,752) and Research Work 2 (Al Riza et al. 2015, residential lighting, LCC = RM 23,132–24,984). The proposed system serves an academic building at 813 kWh/day load — roughly 400× larger energy demand, which explains the LCC of RM 1.5M+. The key contribution is not the absolute cost but the demonstration that RIM can extract 77.8% reliability improvement for just 4.9% cost increase — a finding applicable to all scales.

### 5.5 Key Takeaways

1. **The nonlinear cost-reliability curve is the central insight of this paper.** There are operating points where system reliability can be improved by ~78% with only ~5% cost increase — finding these points requires analyzing the full LPSP landscape, not just optimizing for a fixed target.

2. **The proposed Reliability Improvement Method (RIM) fills a gap in traditional sizing.** Unlike GCM (which ignores lifetime costs) and fixed-LCC optimization (which locks in a target LPSP), RIM identifies where additional reliability investments have the highest marginal return. Starting from a 1% LPSP baseline, the method iteratively checks cost gradients to find the knee point at 0.18% LPSP.

3. **Battery sizing dominates cost sensitivity.** The analysis shows that adding a single PV array can reduce required battery banks by 68 units (78% reduction), saving RM 470,031 in LCC. This underscores that in SAPS, generation investment is often more cost-effective than storage investment for reliability improvement.

4. **The GCM gives suboptimal lifetime costs.** While GCM is computationally fast (tangency method without iteration), it misses O&M costs entirely. The LCC method produces a configuration with RM 1.51M lifetime cost vs the GCM's RM 1.26M apparent cost — a 20% underestimate.

5. **90,000 configurations analyzed with 788.4 billion data points.** The brute-force iterative approach, while computationally expensive, guarantees that the global optimum is found within the discretized search space. This comprehensive enumeration is what enables the isoreliability line visualization and the identification of non-obvious improvement opportunities.
