## SECTION 4: POWER GENERATION ALGORITHM — EXACT EXTRACTION (STRICT EXTRACTION)

### 4.1 Dispatch Strategy

**Type of dispatch:** Hierarchical priority-based energy management with grid interaction.

**Priority order of generation sources:**
1. **Primary:** Renewable sources (PV + WT) — always used first
2. **Secondary:** Fuel cell (using stored hydrogen) — activated when renewable generation < load
3. **Tertiary:** Utility grid — provides remaining deficit after FC, and absorbs surplus after electrolyzer

**Decision logic for when each component turns on/off:**
- **Case 1:** When P_ren(t) × η_inv = P_load(t): Load fully supplied by renewables, fuel cell inactive
- **Case 2:** When P_ren(t) × η_inv > P_load(t): Surplus sent to electrolyzer; any excess beyond electrolyzer capacity exported to grid
- **Case 3:** When P_ren(t) × η_inv < P_load(t): Deficit supplied by fuel cell; remaining shortage covered by grid

**Constraints on component operation:**
- Electrolyzer limited by rated capacity (P_Elz,max) and available hydrogen tank storage
- Fuel cell limited by rated capacity and available hydrogen
- Hydrogen tank operates between mH2_min and mH2_max

### 4.2 Power Flow Logic

**How excess renewable energy is handled:**
1. First priority: Electrolyzer produces hydrogen (up to rated capacity)
2. Second priority: Export surplus to utility grid

**How deficit is handled:**
1. First priority: Fuel cell generates electricity from stored hydrogen
2. Second priority: Import from utility grid

**Battery charging/discharging logic and SOC limits:**
- **No battery in this system.** Storage is entirely hydrogen-based.

**Hydrogen production and consumption logic:**
- Hydrogen produced by electrolyzer: P_H2_produced(t) = min(E_H2_tank_max − E_H2_tank(t−1), P_ren−Elz(t)) × η_Elz
- Hydrogen consumed by fuel cell: P_FC−inv = P_H2_tank−FC(t) × η_FC
- Tank energy balance: E_H2_tank(t) = E_H2_tank(t−1) + P_Elz−H2_tank(t) − P_H2_tank−FC(t)
- Tank mass bounds: mH2_min ≤ m_H2(t) ≤ mH2_max

**Diesel generator start/stop conditions:**
- **No diesel generator in this system.**

**Grid interaction logic:**
- Bidirectional: import when deficit exceeds FC capacity, export when surplus exceeds electrolyzer capacity
- Economic evaluation via fixed tariffs (ρ_imp for imports, ρ_exp for exports)
- Grid fluctuation index (Dgs) monitored: max 33% of installed capacity variation per time interval

### 4.4 Algorithm Flow (if described)

The operational simulation proceeds hourly through the following steps:

1. **Initialize:** Load hourly meteorological data (solar irradiance, wind speed, ambient temperature), load demand, component specifications
2. **For each time step t = 1 to 8,760:**
   - Calculate PV output: P_PV(t) = P_rated_PV × (G(t)/G_ref) × [1 + β(T(t) − T_ref)], then E_PV(t) = N_PV × P_PV(t)
   - Calculate WT output: Convert wind speed to hub height (power-law), then apply piecewise power curve (Equation 4); E_WT(t) = N_WT × P_WT(t)
   - Compute renewable surplus/deficit: P_surp(t) = P_ren(t) × η_inv − P_load(t)
   - **If surplus:** Send to electrolyzer (up to capacity); remainder to grid
   - **If deficit:** Discharge fuel cell (up to capacity/availability); remainder from grid
   - Update hydrogen tank energy balance
3. **After simulation:** Calculate performance indicators:
   - TAC from component costs + grid import/export economics
   - LPSP from hours of unmet load
   - F_grid from max surplus power variation
   - D_load from renewable-load mismatch
   - REF from renewable fraction
   - Penalty cost C_pc from constraint violations
4. **Optimization loop:** PSO iteratively updates component capacities, repeats simulation until convergence (max 100 iterations, 60 particles)
5. **Output:** Configuration minimizing TAC + C_pc

Key governing equation — **power balance:**
> P_inv−load = (P_FC−inv + P_ren−inv) × η_inv

Where:
- P_ren−inv = renewable power to load via inverter
- P_FC−inv = fuel cell power to load via inverter
- P_inv−load = total power supplied to load

## SECTION 5: ANALYTICAL INSIGHT AND SYSTEM EXPLANATION (YOUR ANALYSIS)

### 5.1 Power Generation Walkthrough

**How this system generates and delivers power on a typical day:**

The PV–WT–FC system in Dakhla operates as a grid-connected hybrid where renewables are the primary workhorses, hydrogen provides intraday-to-multiday storage, and the grid serves as both a last-resort backup and an export market for surplus energy.

**Morning (6:00–9:00):** Solar irradiance ramps up from zero; with Dakhla's excellent solar resource, G(t) quickly rises. Wind speeds are often moderate at this time. The 530 kW PV array begins generating, but output may initially fall below the load. The fuel cell — drawing from the 780 kWh / 19.65 kg hydrogen tank — covers the gap. At 100 kW rated FC output, this provides a meaningful but not dominant share. If the FC alone cannot cover the shortfall (e.g., high early-morning demand spike), the grid imports the remainder.

**Midday (10:00–15:00):** Peak solar. PV output reaches several hundred kW. In summer months (June), the paper notes PV generation "often exceeds the load demand during daytime hours." The system now has a significant surplus: P_surp(t) = P_ren(t) × η_inv − P_load(t). This surplus is routed to the 199.1 kW electrolyzer. At rated input, the electrolyzer converts ~199.1 kW of electrical power into hydrogen at the efficiency η_Elz (not stated), storing energy in the tank. If electrolyzer capacity plus tank capacity headroom are exceeded, remaining surplus is exported to the grid — generating revenue at the export tariff ρ_exp.

**Evening (16:00–20:00):** Solar drops rapidly; load often peaks (daily demand cycle). PV output falls to zero by ~18:00–19:00 in Dakhla. Wind power may or may not be available — it's stochastic (2–9 m/s range). The fuel cell, with its 100 kW capacity, ramps up to cover the gap. The 780 kWh hydrogen tank provides enough buffer for several hours of FC operation before depletion. If hydrogen runs low and wind is insufficient, the grid imports cover the difference.

**Night (21:00–5:00):** Zero solar. Wind is the sole renewable source — and in Dakhla, wind speeds of 2–9 m/s indicate moderate nighttime wind. The 140 kW wind capacity (70 turbines at 2 kW each inferred) may produce 0–140 kW depending on instantaneous wind. If wind < load, FC covers the deficit. If FC reaches its 100 kW limit and hydrogen is depleted, the grid provides the rest.

**Seasonal variations:**
- **Summer (June):** High solar irradiance (peaks >800 W/m²), stable wind. PV generation at maximum. FC and grid imports minimized. Maximum hydrogen production and grid export. The paper states this is "near-optimal operating conditions."
- **Winter (January):** "Moderate solar availability" — lower peak PV output. Wind variable. More frequent FC operation and grid imports. Hydrogen tank discharges more and may reach minimum levels during extended low-renewable periods. The system's reliability depends heavily on hydrogen reserves during these periods.

### 5.2 System Behavior Analysis

**Why PV–WT–FC is optimal:**

The WT–FC configuration achieves the lowest LCOE ($0.1164/kWh) but fails on grid stability — its Dgs of 0.376 exceeds the 0.33 constraint. Wind-only systems are inherently more volatile than PV–wind combos because wind fluctuations are larger and less predictable on short timescales. The penalty cost of $700,588 reflects this: failed reliability is catastrophically expensive in this framework. The PV–FC configuration avoids grid instability but at $0.1964/kWh and 60% REF, it's the most expensive and least green option — undersized renewable capacity leads to heavy grid import dependence.

PV–WT–FC achieves **synergia**: PV provides predictable daytime generation; wind complements (especially in Dakhla where wind resource exists); the combination smoothens the aggregate renewable output curve. The result: Dgs = 0.33 (just within bounds), Dload = 0.988 (excellent alignment), REF = 88.32%.

**Relationship between renewable penetration and storage sizing:**

Comparing the three configurations reveals a striking pattern:
- WT–FC (96.54% REF): requires 21,020 kWh H₂ storage (529.47 kg) — extremely large
- PV–FC (60% REF): requires 1,490.5 kWh H₂ storage (37.54 kg) — moderate
- PV–WT–FC (88.32% REF): requires only 780 kWh H₂ storage (19.65 kg) — smallest!

The PV–WT–FC combination's diversified renewable sources reduce the need for massive storage. Wind-only systems need enormous hydrogen buffers to ride through long calm periods; the hybrid system avoids this by having solar cover daytime and wind covering other periods. This is the key insight: **resource complementarity reduces storage requirements.**

**Trade-offs the authors identified:**
1. LCOE minimization alone leads to impractical solutions (WT–FC has lowest LCOE but highest penalty)
2. Grid stability must be explicitly constrained — it doesn't emerge naturally from cost optimization
3. Hydrogen storage adds 26–46% of total system cost but provides essential flexibility and reliability
4. Grid export capability reduces LCOE by ~6% (from $0.1527 to $0.1437) — significant economic benefit

**Edge cases:**
- Extended cloudy periods: Hydrogen tank depletion → FC can't run → grid imports increase
- Low wind + low cloud: Maximum stress on system; FC at full hydrogen-discharge capacity; grid as safety net
- Load growth: Would require re-optimization; current optimal is sized for Dakhla's specific load profile

### 5.3 Critical Evaluation

**Are the assumptions reasonable?**
- Wind shear exponent n = 0.14 is standard for open terrain — reasonable for Dakhla (coastal desert)
- Constant component efficiencies (η_Elz, η_FC, η_inv) simplify reality — actual efficiencies vary with load and temperature
- Fixed grid tariffs ignore time-of-use pricing — could underestimate export revenue potential
- Hourly simulation ignores sub-hourly fluctuations — the paper acknowledges this limitation
- HHV of H₂ = 39.7 kWh/kg is standard and accurate

**Limitations of this study:**
1. No experimental validation — purely simulation-based
2. Component costs referenced from external sources — not provided in the paper itself, making full reproducibility difficult
3. No diesel generator or battery storage in configurations — only hydrogen is considered for storage
4. Optimization uses PSO only — no comparison with other algorithms (GA, WOA, etc.)
5. No sensitivity analysis on discount rate, component cost escalation, or fuel price changes
6. Emission factor value not stated — CO₂ savings cannot be independently verified
7. Load profile for a specific microgrid — may not generalize to other contexts
8. Grid tariff values not stated — limits economic reproducibility

**How generalizable are the findings?**
The framework (penalty-based PSO with grid stability constraints) is generalizable. The specific optimal sizing is site-specific to Dakhla's excellent solar resource, moderate wind, and the particular microgrid load profile. The key finding — that multi-constraint optimization prevents misleading LCOE-only solutions — is broadly applicable.

**What would change if parameters were different?**
- Higher discount rate: Would penalize PV/wind (high upfront cost) less favorably vs. grid imports
- Lower component costs: Would shift optimal toward more renewables and less grid dependence
- Higher grid export tariff: Would favor larger PV/wind and smaller hydrogen storage
- Adding diesel: Would reduce hydrogen storage needs but lower REF
- Larger load: Linear scaling of components approximately, but grid tariff structure matters

**Comparison with similar systems:**
The paper compares against 4 Moroccan studies:
- Bouaouda & Sayouti [36]: LCOE $0.3115/kWh (off-grid PV-WT-FC in Dakhla)
- Sahab et al. [37]: LCOE $0.23/kWh (off-grid PV-WT-FC-GES in Safi)
- Elaouzy et al. [38]: LCOE $0.21/kWh (grid-connected PV-WT-BT-FC in Tangier)
- El Hassani et al. [39]: LCOE $0.0701/kWh (islanded WT-FC-DG-FC, but only 44.57% REF)

This paper's LCOE of $0.1472/kWh with 88.32% REF and 0% LPSP represents a **strong balance** — not the absolute cheapest, but the most balanced across all criteria. The El Hassani study achieves lower LCOE at the cost of >50% non-renewable energy.

### 5.5 Key Takeaways

1. **Grid stability constraints are non-optional in grid-connected optimization.** The WT–FC configuration's $700,588 penalty cost demonstrates that focusing solely on LCOE produces operationally infeasible systems. The penalty-based approach effectively internalizes what would otherwise be "hidden costs" of grid instability.

2. **Resource complementarity (PV + Wind) reduces storage requirements.** The optimal PV–WT–FC system needs only 780 kWh of hydrogen storage vs. 21,020 kWh for WT–FC. Diversified renewables smooth the generation curve more cheaply than oversized storage.

3. **Hydrogen storage is economically substantial (26–46% of total cost) but essential.** Without it, the system cannot bridge the gap between variable renewables and continuous load. The cost is justified by the reliability (LPSP = 0%) it enables.

4. **PV–WT–FC at $0.1472/kWh is competitive for Morocco.** It outperforms reported LCOEs of $0.21–$0.31/kWh for similar systems in other Moroccan cities, and achieves near-perfect reliability with 88% renewable penetration.

5. **Grid export capability improves economics by ~6%.** The ability to sell surplus energy is not trivial — it meaningfully closes the cost gap between renewable hybrid systems and pure grid reliance. This has policy implications: feed-in tariffs can significantly improve HRES viability.
