## SECTION 4: POWER GENERATION ALGORITHM — EXACT EXTRACTION (STRICT EXTRACTION)

### 4.1 Dispatch Strategy

- **DBM method:** Deterministic energy balance — no traditional dispatch strategy per se; instead, sizing is determined so that surplus solar energy (E+) during daylight hours equals or exceeds the nighttime energy deficit (E−) accounting for FC and H₂ tank efficiencies
- **HOMER validation:** **Cycle Charging (CC) control** used — the most suitable controller because it optimally employs the hydrogen system (the other controllers — load following, combined dispatch — do not incorporate the ELZ and hydrogen tank)
- **Priority order:**
  1. Solar PV (primary source, runs whenever irradiance available)
  2. Fuel Cell (runs during nighttime / when solar is insufficient, powered by stored H₂)
  3. Wind turbine (Case Study 2 only, supplementary to PV)
- **Decision logic:** Energy balance condition: E+ ≥ E− = E+ · ηFC · ηH2,Tk — the system is sized so that surplus solar energy during the day, after conversion losses, covers the nighttime deficit

### 4.2 Power Flow Logic

- **Excess renewable energy:** Surplus PV/wind power (when generation > load) is directed to the **Electrolyzer** to produce hydrogen via water electrolysis
- **Hydrogen storage:** Produced H₂ is compressed to 345 bar and stored in the tank (10 × 10 kg tanks + 1 extra) at 98% storage efficiency
- **Energy deficit:** When solar PV cannot meet the load (nighttime, cloudy), the **Fuel Cell** draws stored hydrogen to generate electricity at 90% efficiency
- **Peak shaving:** In December (worst-case month), ~5% deficit is covered by hydrogen stored from surplus months (April–September)
- **No battery** — hydrogen is the sole storage medium
- **No diesel generator** — 100% renewable
- **No grid interaction** — off-grid system

### 4.4 Algorithm Flow (DBM)

The DBM proceeds as follows:

1. **Initialize:** Obtain hourly solar irradiance, wind speed, and temperature data for the location (Cairo) from NASA/METEONORM.
2. **Compute single-module output (E1,PV):** Calculate hourly power output for one 0.335 kW PV module using irradiance and temperature equations (Eqs. 1–5). O(1) operation.
3. **Guess initial NPV:** Start with a random guess for number of PV modules.
4. **Compute daily energy balance for iteration i:**
   - E⁺ = ∫(Ppv − Pl) dt for intervals where Ppv > Pl (surplus)
   - E⁻ = ∫(Pl − Ppv) dt for intervals where Pl > Ppv (deficit) — split into E⁻,₁ (midnight to sunrise) and E⁻,₂ (sunset to midnight)
   - ED = ∫Pl dt for intervals where Ppv > Pl (load met directly by solar)
   - Check constraint: E⁺ ≥ E⁻ / (ηFC · ηH2,Tk) — surplus must be sufficient to cover deficit after FC + tank losses
5. **Iterate NPV:** Repeat step 4 with incremented NPV until energy balance is satisfied. Each iteration is O(1), total O(m) where m is the number of iterations.
6. **Optimal sizing determined:** The smallest integer NPV satisfying the balance is the optimal number.
7. **Size hydrogen system:** Sizing of ELZ, FC, and H₂ tank follows from the maximum surplus energy and the maximum deficit — ELZ sized to handle peak surplus (~23 kg H₂/h), tank sized for maximum seasonal storage (~100 kg), FC sized to cover peak load.
8. **Compute annual energy production:** Integrate hourly outputs over 8,760 h using the same model equations.
9. **Compute LCOE:** Using capital cost (Eq. 13), maintenance cost (Eq. 14), present values (Eqs. 17–22), and annual energy production.

The same procedure applies for wind turbines in Case Study 2 — optimal NW is determined analogously using wind speed data and turbine power curve (Eq. 6).

## SECTION 5: ANALYTICAL INSIGHT AND SYSTEM EXPLANATION (YOUR ANALYSIS)

### 5.1 Power Generation Walkthrough

**System conception.** This is a purely solar-plus-hydrogen (and optionally wind) off-grid microgrid with **no battery** and **no diesel backup** — a bold design that achieves 100% renewable fraction purely through seasonal hydrogen storage. The system serves a community load peaking at 250 kW, based on the IEEE Reliability Test System profile, located at Cairo International Airport.

**A typical sunny day (e.g., June).**

*Morning (6:00–8:00):* Solar irradiance begins to rise. The PV array (5,400 panels = 1,809 kWp) starts generating power. During early morning, load is relatively low (below ~100 kW). Since PV output is still below load, the deficit (E⁻) is covered by the **Fuel Cell** drawing stored hydrogen from the previous day's surplus. The FC operates at 90% efficiency, consuming ~5,800 Btu/kWh.

*Midday (10:00–14:00):* Peak solar hours. PV output reaches up to ~1,800 kW — far exceeding the load (~180 kW average, peak 250 kW). This massive surplus (E⁺) is directed entirely to the **Electrolyzer** (300 kW nominal, expanded to handle the full surplus). At 80% efficiency consuming 5 kWh/Nm³, the ELZ produces hydrogen at a rate up to 23 kg/h. Over the summer months June–August, daily net PV generation averages ~274,000–279,000 kWh/month, or ~9,000 kWh/day — about 6× the daily load.

*Evening (18:00–22:00):* Solar irradiance drops to zero. The load peaks (possibly reaching the 250 kW peak around 19:00 as per RTS profile). The **Fuel Cell** ramps up to deliver this peak demand, drawing from the day's hydrogen production. The FC operational range is 0–250 kW (per HOMER profiles).

*Night (22:00–6:00):* Load drops to a minimum (RTS nighttime valley ~50–60% of peak, ~125–150 kW). The FC continues operating at partial load to cover this baseload. H₂ stored during the day is consumed through the night.

**Seasonal dynamics.** The paper reveals a crucial seasonal pattern:

- **Summer (April–September):** High irradiance + moderate temperatures → H₂ **production exceeds consumption**. Hydrogen accumulates in the 100 kg tank, reaching up to full capacity.
- **Winter (October–March):** Lower irradiance + higher temperature-related losses (actually temperature losses are higher in summer! Wait — losses are 4.21% in Jan vs 10.18% in Jul, but the net energy is much higher in summer due to higher irradiance) → H₂ **consumption exceeds production**. The tank is drawn down.
- **December (worst month):** Only 118,755 kWh net PV energy — ~5% below what the load requires. This deficit is covered by **drawing on hydrogen stored since summer**. The seasonal round-trip efficiency through H₂ production → storage → FC reconversion is ηELZ × ηtank × ηFC = 0.80 × 0.98 × 0.90 = **~70.6%**.

**The system produces ~2,000 kg of surplus hydrogen per year** that could be exported — potentially for fuel cell vehicles or other uses — demonstrating that this configuration not only meets its own load but produces a net energy storage surplus.

### 5.2 System Behavior Analysis

**Why 5,400 panels?** The DBM finds the minimum number of PV modules that guarantees the energy balance constraint: E⁺ ≥ E⁻ / (ηFC · ηtank). Each SunPower panel produces ~455 kWh/year (derived: 2,456,288 kWh annual net / 5,400 panels), so 1,809 kWp of PV generates ~2.46 GWh/year, of which ~1.58 GWh serves the load directly and ~0.88 GWh powers the electrolyzer. The sizing is a direct consequence of Cairo's solar resource and the 70.6% round-trip efficiency of the hydrogen pathway.

**Why no battery?** The paper relies entirely on hydrogen for energy storage. This is economical only because the Mediterranean climate provides strong seasonal differentiation — long summer days produce far more solar energy than needed, which is converted to hydrogen and stored for winter. Battery storage would be impractical for multi-month seasonal shifting at this scale. However, it also means the system depends heavily on the FC being available for **every single night** — 4,380 hours of nightly FC operation per year — which has implications for FC lifetime and maintenance costs ($816,934 total maintenance for the FC).

**The wind addition (Case Study 2).** Adding wind turbines reduces LCOE from $0.247 to $0.2116 — a **14.3% reduction**. The analysis compares configurations ranging from 250 kW to 750 kW WTG capacity. The optimal configuration has high solar PV penetration with supplementary wind. This makes sense for Cairo: winter months have lower solar but potentially higher wind speeds, smoothing the seasonal profile and reducing the required hydrogen storage cycling. The HOMER validation, however, showed that HOMER favors wind over solar, leading to lower wind turbine capacity factors — suggesting the DBM's solar-heavy optimal configuration may be more economically sensible.

**Why is NPV negative?** Both cases show negative NPV at ESP = $0.0788/kWh (the Egyptian government's solar PV feed-in tariff). This means the system is **not profitable** as a standalone project at current electricity prices. The LCOE ($0.2116–$0.247/kWh) is 2.7–3.1× the selling price. This quantifies an important reality: hydrogen-based seasonal storage adds significant cost. The value proposition must come from either:
- Off-grid applications where diesel alternatives are more expensive (remote communities, airports)
- Future scenarios with higher carbon prices or lower hydrogen equipment costs
- Monetizing the hydrogen itself (~2,000 kg/year surplus) as a transport fuel

**Computational efficiency breakthrough.** HOMER required ~6 hours (Case Study 1) to 14 hours (Case Study 2) on modest hardware (i5, 4 GB RAM). DBM completes in **minutes** with O(m) linear complexity. This is significant for sensitivity analysis — the authors could evaluate hundreds of scenarios in the time HOMER evaluates one.

### 5.3 Critical Evaluation

**Strengths:**
- The DBM's **convexity proof** (Section "Global optimality and computational complexity analysis") is a strong theoretical contribution — it guarantees that the solution is globally optimal, not just locally optimal as with metaheuristics
- The **linear-time O(m) complexity** is a genuine advance over the exponential or polynomial complexity of GA, PSO, and other metaheuristics
- **Verification against HOMER Pro** (industry-standard commercial software) with <5% deviation adds credibility
- 100% renewable fraction without diesel backup is ambitious and demonstrated
- The **monthly energy balance data** (Table 3) provides transparency into seasonal dynamics

**Limitations:**
- **Uncertainty not addressed.** The paper acknowledges this: "despite the method designation, the DBM does not prioritize addressing uncertainties." Real solar and wind output varies year-to-year; the 1-year simulation based on a single meteorological year may not capture worst-case multi-year droughts
- **No battery comparison.** The paper does not compare against PV+battery or PV+wind+battery configurations, which might be more economical for diurnal (daily) cycling even if impractical for seasonal storage
- **Component sizing ambiguity.** The FC is listed as 250 kW in Table 5 but 300 kW in the Case Study 1 text; ELZ is listed with "800 kW and 1000 kW, respectively" which is ambiguous (two scenarios? both?)
- **HOMER number discrepancy.** "The hybrid power system derives its electrical production primarily from the solar PV system, contributing 76.2%, and the Genset (fuel cell), providing 23.8%, or 748,235 kWh/year" — if 748,235 is the FC output, then total is ~3.14 GWh. But the load (1.58 GWh) + ELZ (1.06 GWh) sums to 2.65 GWh. The ~0.5 GWh difference may be excess electricity, but the text separately reports excess as 366,370 kWh/year. These numbers don't perfectly reconcile.
- **System lifetime.** The H₂ tank is rated for 15 years but the project lifetime is 25 years — a replacement cost for the tank ($2,000 every 15 years) would be needed but is not listed
- **Single location.** The study tests only Cairo, Egypt. Generalizability to other latitudes and climate zones would require replication

### 5.5 Key Takeaways

1. **DBM is a breakthrough in computational efficiency.** It reduces hybrid system sizing from hours (HOMER: 6–14 h) to minutes, with O(m) linear complexity and a convexity proof guaranteeing global optimality — all while achieving <5% deviation from HOMER results.

2. **100% renewable is achievable with hydrogen-only storage, but expensive.** The system serves a 250 kW peak load purely from PV + H₂ (or PV + Wind + H₂) with no diesel backup. However, the LCOE ($0.2116–$0.247/kWh) is 2.7–3.1× the Egyptian solar feed-in tariff ($0.0788/kWh), making the system uneconomical for grid-tied applications. The value proposition is for **off-grid premium applications** where diesel alternatives are more expensive or unavailable.

3. **Adding wind reduces LCOE by 14%.** The PV+Wind+H₂ configuration ($0.2116/kWh) outperforms the PV-only H₂ system ($0.247/kWh), confirming the value of resource diversification. However, HOMER's preference for wind over solar may lead to lower turbine capacity factors.

4. **Seasonal hydrogen storage works, with ~70.6% round-trip efficiency.** The system produces ~2,000 kg/year of surplus hydrogen, converting summer solar surplus into winter energy. The seasonal storage cycle operates at 70.6% efficiency (80% ELZ × 98% tank × 90% FC), which is competitive for seasonal storage but significantly lower than battery round-trip efficiency (~85–95%).

5. **NPV is negative at current tariffs — the economics need a hydrogen value stream.** The system generates ~2,000 kg/year of surplus hydrogen that could be sold as transport fuel or industrial feedstock. Monetizing this hydrogen, combined with falling electrolyzer and FC costs, could make the system viable. The paper implicitly argues that hydrogen-based seasonal storage requires either higher electricity prices, carbon pricing, or a hydrogen market to break even.
