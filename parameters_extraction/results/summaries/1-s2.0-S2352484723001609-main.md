## SECTION 4: POWER GENERATION ALGORITHM — EXACT EXTRACTION (STRICT EXTRACTION)

### 4.1 Dispatch Strategy

- **Type of dispatch:** HOMER Pro optimized dispatch (cycle following / load following hybrid)
- **Priority order of generation sources:**
  1. Existing wind turbines on Thursday Island (provide ~10% of demand)
  2. Hydrogen fuel cells on Thursday Island (provide remaining 90% in 100% RE scenario)
  3. Diesel generators (backup only in 100% RE; 20% or 50% of demand in other scenarios)
- **Decision logic:** HOMER determines optimal dispatch at each time step to minimize total NPC while meeting the electrical load and hydrogen production requirements
- **Constraints on component operation:** Electrolyser minimum load ratio = 35%; diesel gensets have 40,000 hours remaining life

### 4.2 Power Flow Logic

**Horn Island (Hydrogen Production):**
- Solar PV and wind generators produce electricity on Horn Island
- Excess renewable energy powers the electrolyser to produce hydrogen
- Hydrogen is stored in tanks
- Hydrogen is transported via subsea pipeline to Thursday Island

**Thursday Island (Electricity Generation):**
- Existing wind turbines meet ~10% of electrical demand
- Fuel cells convert hydrogen to electricity for the remaining demand (90% in 100% RE scenario)
- Diesel generators provide backup or supplementary power (depending on scenario)
- Converters convert DC fuel cell output to AC for the distribution network

**Excess energy handling:** Excess electricity on Horn Island is used for hydrogen production; the system experiences high excess electricity percentages (72% in 100% RE 0% unmet scenario) due to the need to guarantee hydrogen production under all weather conditions

**Deficit handling:** In scenarios with unmet hydrogen allowance, diesel generators on Thursday Island compensate for hydrogen shortfall

### 4.3 Control Parameters

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Electrolyser minimum load ratio | 35% |
| Fuel cell efficiency | 60% |
| Converter efficiency | 95% |
| Solar PV derating factor | 90% |
| Hydrogen energy density | 33.36 kWh/kg |

### 4.4 Algorithm Flow

The paper describes the HOMER Pro modeling process (Fig. 10):
1. Input weather data (solar, wind, temperature), location data, load demand, available resources, economic parameters
2. HOMER calculates optimized component sizes and least-cost solution
3. Sensitivity analysis varies input parameters to assess impact on outputs
4. Two separate models: Horn Island (hydrogen production) and Thursday Island (electricity generation)
5. Results integrated to obtain overall system LCOE and LCOH

**Power balance equation (implicit in HOMER):**
- At each time step: Generation (Wind_FC + Diesel + Wind_existing) = Load demand
- Horn Island: PV + Wind → Electrolyser → H2 storage → Pipeline → Thursday Island

## SECTION 5: ANALYTICAL INSIGHT AND SYSTEM EXPLANATION (YOUR ANALYSIS)

### 5.1 Power Generation Walkthrough

This system operates as a two-island energy hub: hydrogen is produced on Horn Island (the larger, sparsely populated neighbor) and transported via subsea pipeline to Thursday Island (the densely populated demand center) for electricity generation in fuel cells.

**Morning (6:00–9:00):**
Solar irradiance begins to ramp up on Horn Island. The PV arrays (30 MW in the 100% RE scenario) start generating, but output is modest. Wind speeds are typically moderate in the early morning (April–October windy season). The electrolyser begins drawing power as solar output exceeds its minimum 35% load threshold. Hydrogen production ramps up gradually. On Thursday Island, the existing 450 kW wind turbines contribute their modest morning output (~5–10% of the 2,820.5 kW average load), and the fuel cell draws from stored hydrogen to meet the morning load.

**Midday (10:00–14:00):**
Solar irradiance peaks on Horn Island. With 30 MW of PV at 16% efficiency and 90% derating, peak output could reach approximately 30 × 0.16 × 0.9 × peak_sun_hours ≈ significant power generation. The 15 MW electrolyser operates at or near full capacity. Excess electricity beyond electrolyser capacity is substantial (72% excess in the 0% unmet scenario), reflecting the oversizing needed to guarantee hydrogen production during low-resource periods. The hydrogen tank charges, storing hydrogen for evening/nighttime use. On Thursday Island, the 4.5 MW fuel cell operates at high output, converting hydrogen to electricity at 60% efficiency to meet the daytime load of approximately 2,820.5 kW average (with the fuel cell providing ~2,538 kW after wind contributes ~282 kW).

**Evening (17:00–21:00):**
Solar output drops to zero as sunset occurs. Wind may still be available (especially during April–October). The electrolyser reduces output or shuts down if renewable generation falls below the 35% minimum load threshold. On Thursday Island, the fuel cell continues drawing from hydrogen storage to meet the evening peak demand (up to 4,062 kW in summer). The hydrogen tank level begins to decline as stored hydrogen is consumed.

**Night (22:00–5:00):**
No solar generation. Wind is the sole renewable source on Horn Island. If wind is insufficient, hydrogen production stops entirely. On Thursday Island, the fuel cell continues operating from stored hydrogen. The 4,000 kg hydrogen tank (in the 100% RE scenario) must contain enough stored hydrogen to bridge extended low-wind, low-solar periods. The paper shows (Fig. 13) that storage levels fluctuate significantly, dropping toward zero during poor weather.

**Seasonal variations:**
- August–December: Higher solar radiation on Horn Island → increased hydrogen production
- April–October: Higher wind speeds → increased hydrogen production
- The complementary seasonal patterns of solar and wind help maintain year-round hydrogen production
- Summer brings peak electrical demand (up to 4,062 kW) on Thursday Island due to cooling loads

### 5.2 System Behavior Analysis

**Why this configuration was chosen as optimal:**
The 80% renewable scenario emerges as the most cost-effective solution. It achieves an LCOE of AU$0.78/kWh (at 0% unmet H2) which drops to AU$0.41–0.48/kWh when 10–20% unmet hydrogen is allowed. This is below the existing diesel-based LCOE of AU$0.512/kWh. The 100% renewable scenario is prohibitively expensive at AU$1.08/kWh (0% unmet) because the system must be massively oversized to guarantee hydrogen production during the worst weather conditions, resulting in 72% excess electricity.

**Relationship between renewable penetration and storage sizing:**
Higher renewable penetration requires disproportionately larger component sizing. Going from 50% to 100% renewable requires doubling the PV capacity (10→30 MW) and more than doubling wind capacity (15→33 MW). The hydrogen tank also grows from 3,000 to 4,000 kg. This non-linear scaling is because the system must meet demand during the worst renewable periods, not just the average.

**How the dispatch strategy affects component lifetimes:**
The fuel cell has a 50,000-hour lifetime. At full load (4.5 MW), this equates to approximately 11.1 years of continuous operation. In practice, with variable loading, the lifetime may extend to 15–20 years. The electrolyser's 15-year lifetime aligns with typical project replacement cycles. The high excess electricity (72%) means the electrolyser frequently operates at partial load or is curtailed, which may actually extend its lifetime but reduces economic efficiency.

**Trade-offs identified by the authors:**
1. **Cost vs. emissions:** 100% renewable is cleanest (almost 0 CO2) but most expensive (AU$1.08/kWh). 50% renewable is cheapest (AU$0.59/kWh) but still emits 3.9M kg CO2/year.
2. **Reliability vs. cost:** Allowing 20% unmet hydrogen dramatically reduces LCOE (from AU$1.08 to AU$0.50 for 100% RE) by enabling smaller components, but requires keeping diesel generators as backup.
3. **Land use vs. system feasibility:** Thursday Island's 3.5 km² area cannot accommodate utility-scale RE, necessitating the two-island approach.

**Edge cases:**
- Extended cloudy/calm periods: Hydrogen storage depletes; diesel generators must compensate (if unmet H2 > 0% is allowed)
- Load growth: Would require additional fuel cell capacity and hydrogen production infrastructure
- Electrolyser failure: Complete loss of hydrogen production; full reliance on diesel backup

### 5.3 Critical Evaluation

**Reasonableness of assumptions:**
The assumption that 10% of demand is met by existing wind is reasonable given the 450 kW capacity relative to the 2,820.5 kW average load. The AU$1.3/litre diesel price is realistic for remote Australian locations. The 4.07% discount rate is appropriate for Australian infrastructure projects. However, assuming zero CAPEX for existing diesel generators is a simplification — while their capital cost is sunk, their operating costs are substantial.

**Limitations of this study:**
1. The authors acknowledge that component cost uncertainties were not considered
2. Government policies and subsidies significantly impact LCOE/LCOH but were not assessed
3. No financial modeling of importing hydrogen vs. local production
4. The cost of hydrogen transportation by small vessels (potentially more realistic than pipelines for 3 km) could not be estimated due to lack of data
5. The study assumes perfect forecasting of hydrogen demand; real-world dispatch would face uncertainty
6. Environmental approval for subsea pipelines in the Great Barrier Reef region may be as challenging as undersea cables

**Generalizability:**
The two-island hydrogen hub model is highly applicable to:
- Indonesian archipelago (thousands of small islands)
- Philippines island communities
- Pacific Island nations
- Any remote island group where one island has land for RE and another has the demand center

**What would change if parameters were different:**
- If electrolyser costs drop to AU$1,000/kW (projected by some forecasts), the 100% RE scenario could approach AU$0.50/kWh
- If carbon pricing is introduced (e.g., AU$50/tonne CO2), the diesel baseline LCOE would increase, making hydrogen more competitive
- If PV tracking is eliminated, system costs increase (35 MW PV needed vs. 30 MW for 100% scenario)

**Comparison to similar systems:**
The comparative assessment (Table 23) shows the proposed model achieves AU$0.78/kWh vs. AU$0.80–0.83/kWh for comparable systems in the literature (Vera et al., 2020; Clairand et al., 2019), demonstrating competitive performance for the 80% renewable scenario.

### 5.4 Derived/Inferred Values

**Average daily generation per source (100% RE scenario, derived):**
- Total daily electricity needed for H2 production: 60,930 kWh (for electrolyser) + conversion losses
- At 80% electrolyser efficiency: Energy per kg H2 = 39.4/0.8 = 49.25 kWh/kg
- Daily H2 production: 3,044.1 kg → Energy input = 3,044.1 × 49.25 = 149,922 kWh/day
- This exceeds the stated 60,930 kWh because the 60,930 kWh is the electrical load on Thursday Island, not the total energy input to Horn Island
- Total Horn Island generation ≈ 149,922 kWh/day (to produce 3,044.1 kg H2)
- PV contribution (30 MW, ~5.5 peak sun hours avg): ~30,000 × 5.5 × 0.16 × 0.9 = 23,760 kWh/day
- Wind contribution (33 MW, ~30% capacity factor): ~33,000 × 24 × 0.30 = 237,600 kWh/day
- Total generation ≈ 261,360 kWh/day → Excess = (261,360 - 149,922)/261,360 ≈ 42.6% (paper states 72% for 0% unmet, suggesting different capacity factors)

**Capacity factors (derived from stated capacities and loads):**
- Fuel cell (4.5 MW, 100% RE): Annual H2 consumption = 1,270,607 kg
- Energy output per kg H2 in fuel cell: 33.36 kWh/kg × 0.60 efficiency = 20.016 kWh/kg
- Annual generation from FC: 1,270,607 × 20.016 = 25,432,000 kWh/year
- FC capacity factor: 25,432,000 / (4,500 × 8,760) = 25,432,000 / 39,420,000 = 64.5%

**Storage autonomy:**
- 4,000 kg H2 tank × 20.016 kWh/kg = 80,064 kWh of electrical energy storage
- At average load of 2,820.5 kW: 80,064 / 2,820.5 = 28.4 hours of full-load autonomy
- At 90% of load (2,538.5 kW, after wind): 80,064 / 2,538.5 = 31.5 hours

**Cost breakdown (100% RE, 0% unmet):**
- Total NPC (Thursday Island): AU$122M
- Total NPCh (Horn Island): AU$431M
- Combined NPC: AU$553M
- Horn Island share: 77.9% of total cost
- Thursday Island share: 22.1% of total cost
- This shows the hydrogen production infrastructure dominates system economics

**LCOE comparison to existing system:**
- Existing: AU$0.512/kWh
- Best H2 case (50% RE, 20% unmet): AU$0.37/kWh (28% cheaper)
- Worst H2 case (100% RE, 0% unmet): AU$1.08/kWh (111% more expensive)
- The hydrogen system can be cheaper than diesel IF unmet hydrogen is allowed and diesel remains as backup

### 5.5 Key Takeaways

1. **Two-island hydrogen architecture solves land constraints:** By placing electrolysers and RE on Horn Island and fuel cells on Thursday Island, the system overcomes the fundamental barrier of limited land on small islands — a model applicable to thousands of island communities globally.

2. **80% renewable is the sweet spot:** The 80% renewable scenario offers the best balance of cost (AU$0.41–0.78/kWh), emissions reduction (97% CO2 reduction), and practical feasibility. The 100% renewable scenario is prohibitively expensive due to massive oversizing requirements.

3. **Unmet hydrogen tolerance dramatically reduces costs:** Allowing 20% unmet hydrogen production (with diesel backup) reduces the LCOE from AU$1.08 to AU$0.50/kWh in the 100% RE scenario — a 54% cost reduction. This is because the system doesn't need to be sized for worst-case weather conditions.

4. **Hydrogen production dominates costs:** 78% of the total system cost is on the Horn Island (hydrogen production) side. Reducing electrolyser costs, improving efficiency, or finding cheaper renewable energy directly impacts overall viability.

5. **The system can already beat diesel costs:** At AU$0.37–0.48/kWh (with unmet hydrogen), the hydrogen system is cheaper than the existing AU$0.512/kWh diesel-based system, demonstrating that green hydrogen is not just environmentally superior but can be economically competitive for remote island microgrids.
