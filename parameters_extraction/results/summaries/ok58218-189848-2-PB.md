### 4.1 Dispatch Strategy

- **Type of dispatch:** HOMER's built-in optimization dispatch — the software simulates all possible component combinations and operating strategies, sorting results by NPC
- **Priority order:** Not explicitly defined by the user; HOMER's algorithm determines optimal dispatch based on component costs, resource availability, and load requirements

### 4.4 Algorithm Flow

HOMER's optimization process follows this logic:

1. **Search Space Definition:** HOMER defines all possible combinations of component sizes (PV capacity, wind turbine count, battery count, electrolyzer capacity, etc.)
2. **Simulation:** For each combination, HOMER runs hourly simulations for the entire year using meteorological data (solar radiation, wind speed)
3. **Optimization:** The software evaluates each system configuration's technical feasibility and economic viability
4. **Sorting:** Results are sorted from lowest NPC to highest NPC
5. **Convergence:** The optimizer uses a focus factor of 50 to concentrate search near promising configurations
6. **Output:** The system with lowest NPC that meets all constraints is selected as the winner

Key equations governing system operation:

- **PV output:** P_PV = F_PV × C_PV × (G_T / G_T,STC) where F_PV = derating factor, C_PV = rated capacity, G_T = incident radiation, G_T,STC = 1000 W/m²
- **Wind turbine output:** P_wind = (ρ/ρ₀) × P_wind,STP with air density correction
- **Compressor power:** P_comp = (Cp,H2 / η_c) × [(p2/p1)^((r-1)/r) - 1] × m_c
- **Storage tank pressure:** P_ht = n_ht × (R × T_ht) / V_ht
- **Autonomy parameter:** A_h,tank = (E_h,tank × LHV_H2 × 24 h/d) / (L_prem,avg × 3.6 MJ/kWh)
- **LCOE:** C_ann,tot / E_load,served
- **LCOH:** (C_ann,total - C_elec) / M_H2,regen
- **NPC:** C_ann,tot / CRF where CRF = i(1+i)^n / ((1+i)^n - 1)

## SECTION 5: ANALYTICAL INSIGHT AND SYSTEM EXPLANATION

### 5.1 Power Generation Walkthrough

This hydrogen refueling station operates as a fully renewable, off-grid facility designed to serve 25 Toyota Mirai fuel cell vehicles daily. Here is how the system operates through a typical day:

**Early Morning (6:00–8:00):** Solar radiation is low or absent. Wind may be available depending on season and location. The battery begins the day at some state of charge from overnight operations. If wind is sufficient (above 3 m/s cut-in), the Enercon E-53 turbine begins generating. Any residual hydrogen demand from overnight (2 kg/h baseline) is met from the storage tank. The electrolyzer remains off due to insufficient power.

**Morning (8:00–12:00):** Solar radiation ramps up. PV panels begin generating significant power. With 320–419 panels (118–155 kW rated), the array produces increasing power as the sun rises. The electrical load of 443 kWh/day translates to roughly 37 kW average, but the station's primary electrical load is the electrolyzer. During morning hours, PV power first meets the station's parasitic loads (compressor, cooling, controls), then charges batteries, and any surplus drives the PEM electrolyzer to produce hydrogen.

**Midday (12:00–14:00):** This is peak solar generation AND peak hydrogen demand (vehicles refueling). The PV array produces maximum power. The system faces its critical balancing act: hydrogen demand peaks at the same time solar generation peaks. The electrolyzer runs at full capacity (100 kW or 200 kW depending on scenario), consuming most of the PV output. Batteries may charge from any excess or discharge if the electrolyzer cannot absorb all generation. The compressor works to raise hydrogen from 14 bar (electrolyzer output) to 350–700 bar for storage.

**Afternoon (14:00–18:00):** Solar generation declines but remains substantial. Hydrogen demand decreases after the lunch peak. Excess power continues to charge batteries and produce hydrogen. The system builds hydrogen inventory for evening and overnight demand.

**Evening/Night (18:00–6:00):** Solar generation drops to zero. Wind becomes the primary generation source (if present in Scenarios 1 and 2). Battery discharge meets the station's electrical loads. Hydrogen is drawn from the storage tank to meet the overnight baseline demand of 2 kg/h. If wind is insufficient and batteries reach their 40% minimum SOC, the system cannot meet load — but the optimized configurations are sized to prevent this.

**Seasonal Variations:** Summer months (July–August) provide peak solar radiation, making Scenario 3 (PV-only) particularly effective. Winter months (November–February) reduce solar output but increase wind availability, which is why Scenario 1 (PV+Wind) shows more consistent performance across seasons. The 35° tilt angle optimizes for France's latitude, maximizing annual yield rather than peak summer performance.

### 5.2 System Behavior Analysis

**Why Scenario 3 (PV-Battery) wins:** The analysis clearly shows that PV-only systems with battery storage are the most economical choice across all 20 French cities. This makes intuitive sense: France receives adequate solar radiation (3.535 kWh/m²/day average), PV panels have become relatively inexpensive ($640/kW capital cost with 30-year lifetime), and the daily hydrogen demand profile (peak at midday) aligns well with solar generation patterns. Wind turbines, while valuable in winter, add significant capital cost ($1,000/kW for 800 kW) and the average wind speeds (5.991 m/s annually) are not consistently high enough to justify the investment across all locations.

**The Grenoble anomaly:** Grenoble (C14) shows extreme costs in Scenario 2 (LCOH = $140/kg, NPC = $9.15M) because it requires 9 wind turbines and 219 batteries — the wind resource is poor at this inland Alpine location, and the system must be massively oversized to meet demand. This demonstrates that wind-dominant systems are highly sensitive to local wind resources.

**The Marseille advantage:** Marseille (C17) achieves the lowest costs (LCOH = $13.5/kg, NPC = $886,464) due to its Mediterranean climate with solar yield exceeding 4.3 kWh/m²/day. The 366 PV panels + 437 batteries configuration maximizes solar capture and storage.

**Storage sizing logic:** Battery counts vary dramatically (33–510) across cities and scenarios. More batteries provide greater autonomy but at $110/kWh capital cost with only 10-year replacement life. The optimizer balances battery cost against the reliability requirement to meet the 125 kg/day hydrogen demand.

**Electrolyzer sizing:** The 100 kW electrolyzer is standard across most configurations, with 200 kW used in Scenario 3 for cities with higher solar resources. The 85% efficiency (HHV) means approximately 15% of energy is lost in the electrolysis process.

### 5.3 Critical Evaluation

**Reasonable assumptions:**
- The 25-vehicle/day demand (125 kg H2/day) is a reasonable pilot-scale station
- Component costs are within published ranges for 2022
- The 3.92% real discount rate is reasonable for European infrastructure projects
- NASA meteorological data is a standard resource for HOMER simulations

**Limitations:**
- The study assumes identical load profiles for all 20 cities, ignoring regional driving pattern differences
- No sensitivity analysis on component costs, discount rates, or hydrogen demand growth
- The Toyota Mirai with 5 kg tank and 500 km range is one specific vehicle model; results may differ for buses or trucks requiring more hydrogen
- No degradation modeling for PV panels or batteries over the 25-year lifetime (beyond the derating factor)
- The study does not consider grid connection as a backup option
- No discussion of water supply for electrolysis (approximately 9 liters per kg of H2)
- The dispenser cost ($54,000/kW) appears unusually high and may dominate station economics

**Generalizability:** The findings are most applicable to Southern European locations with similar solar resources. Northern European or tropical locations would likely show different optimal configurations. The PV-Battery dominance is specific to locations where solar resources are adequate and wind resources are moderate.

### 5.5 Key Takeaways

1. **PV-Battery is the winning configuration for French hydrogen refueling stations:** Across all 20 cities studied, the PV-Battery scenario (Scenario 3) consistently delivers the lowest NPC ($886K–$1.09M) and LCOE ($0.354–$0.435/kWh), making solar the most economically viable renewable resource for hydrogen production in France.

2. **Geographic location dramatically affects economics:** Marseille achieves LCOH of $13.5/kg while Grenoble's wind-dependent scenario reaches $140/kg — a 10× difference. Site selection based on renewable resource availability is critical for project viability.

3. **Green hydrogen remains expensive compared to conventional alternatives:** At $13.5–16.5/kg (best case), green hydrogen is significantly more expensive than grey hydrogen (~$1–2/kg from steam reforming) or gasoline on a per-km basis, though it offers zero-emission transportation.

4. **Wind energy is not economically justified in most French locations:** Scenario 2 (wind-battery) is consistently the most expensive option with average NPC of $2.52M and LCOH of $36.54/kg, primarily due to the high capital cost of the 800 kW Enercon turbine and France's moderate average wind speeds (5.991 m/s).

5. **The system is sized for reliability over efficiency:** With battery storage providing ~16 hours of autonomy and electrolyzer capacity factors around 50%, the system prioritizes meeting the daily hydrogen demand over maximizing component utilization — a sensible trade-off for critical refueling infrastructure.
